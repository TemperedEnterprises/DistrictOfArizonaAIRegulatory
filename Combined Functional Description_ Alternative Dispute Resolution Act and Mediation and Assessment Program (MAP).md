### **I. Executive Summary**

The Alternative Dispute Resolution Act of 1998, codified at 28 U.S.C. §§ 651-658, mandates that each United States district court authorize the use of **alternative dispute resolution (ADR)** processes in civil cases. The Act promotes settlement efficiency, reduces litigation burdens, and reinforces judicial economy by integrating structured non-judicial conflict resolution options.

### **II. Legal Mandate and Scope**

Under §651, every federal district court must implement a local ADR program and provide litigants with access to at least one ADR process, typically **mediation, early neutral evaluation (ENE), or arbitration**. The statute applies to most civil actions except habeas corpus or actions in which jurisdiction is vested exclusively in a federal tribunal.

### **III. Functional Objectives of ADR under the Act**

1. Increase **access to justice** by providing time-efficient and cost-effective resolution mechanisms.  
2. Reduce **court congestion** by resolving matters pre-trial.  
3. Foster collaborative, non-adversarial outcomes that may preserve ongoing relationships between parties.  
4. Support **judicial discretion** to compel participation in ADR where appropriate.

### **IV. Integration with District of Arizona Procedures**

The District of Arizona complies with the ADR Act through Local Rule LRCiv 16.2, which governs mediation and judicial settlement conferences. Judges may refer cases to ADR sua sponte or upon motion. Litigants are expected to comply with scheduling orders directing ADR involvement, and **confidentiality is protected** under local enforcement of §652(d).

### **V. Procedural Framework**

* **Initial Case Management:** ADR evaluation discussed at Rule 26(f) conference.  
* **Scheduling Order:** Judges may designate deadlines for ADR referral.  
* **Neutral Appointment:** Parties may agree or court may assign certified mediators or evaluators.  
* **Reporting and Confidentiality:** Outcomes reported under §651(b), with confidentiality protected.

### **VI. Ethical and Regulatory Implications**

* **Confidentiality:** Neutrals and parties are bound by strict confidentiality rules.  
* **Good Faith Participation:** While not always mandatory, courts may enforce participation where ordered.  
* **Pro Se Considerations:** The ADR framework must accommodate unrepresented parties through simplified protocols and clear notice.

### **I. Executive Summary**

The **Mediation and Assessment Program (MAP)** is a judicially integrated, digitally administered court system that expands the principles of the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act to include technology-driven adjudication under a specialized framework. The MAP architecture mirrors Article III courts while functioning entirely online, with structured roles and enhanced procedural access for pro se and disabled litigants. This document outlines the structure, ethical mandates, technical infrastructure, and jurisdiction of MAP.

### **II. Purpose and Jurisdiction**

MAP shall operate as a **digital-first "Small Claims" court** encompassing not only traditional low-value claims (e.g., contract defaults, damage assessments) but also technologically traceable harms and ADA compliance cases under 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq. The system is built to enhance equitable access, streamline conflict resolution, and reduce procedural delays using **Rapid Application Development (RAD)** programming paradigms.

### **III. Confidentiality and Judicial Regulation**

All filings in MAP shall be **public by default**. Confidentiality may only be conferred by explicit order to seal the case issued by a judge. This ensures transparency, accountability, and public access to court activities unless sealing is specifically warranted and ordered.
